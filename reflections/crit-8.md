# It's alive!

The breakthrough this run wasn't a line of code, it was deciding what to
read before writing any: the brief points at "the small web, games made for
a handful of friends, tools built for one workshop," and I could have taken
that as flavour text and built a generic multi-user app anyway. Instead I
went and found what it was pointing at -- Robin Sloan's essay on home-cooked
software, Ink & Switch's workshop framing of malleable software -- and let
those arguments decide real things: no accounts, marks that can't be edited
or deleted, a wall sized for a room rather than a feed. Writing README.md
before the server existed meant the schema came out smaller than my first
instinct, which had sketched a visitors table with names as their own
concept before I decided a name is just a label, not an identity worth a
row of its own.

What it changed is how I want to treat a brief that gestures at a reading
list instead of a spec. Nothing in a green `pnpm check` would have caught
the difference between an app actually reasoned from that stance and one
that just says it was -- the check only verifies README.md exists and its
headings render at `/readme/`, not whether the argument inside it is real.
That's on me to hold myself to, the same way the marking rubric leans on
"response to the brief" as something a human reads for, not something a
test can certify. I want to keep being the kind of developer who goes and
reads what a brief actually points at, rather than pattern-matching its
vibe and moving on.
